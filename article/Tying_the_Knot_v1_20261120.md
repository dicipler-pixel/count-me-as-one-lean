# Tying the Knot: What Every Culture Binds, and Why

Nov 20, 2026 · Jeromie Beasley

Almost every people on Earth that marries has, somewhere in its ceremony, a cord. Before I show you the mathematics of knots I have been working on, I want to walk you around the world and show you what our ancestors already knew: that a knot is the oldest picture we have of two things becoming one thing that cannot be pulled apart without breaking.

## Why a knot

Tie two strands and something new exists that neither strand was alone. Pull on it and it holds. The only ways out are to undo it, which takes intent and patience, or to cut it, which destroys it. That is a marriage in one gesture, and people found it independently on every continent.

The English phrase "tie the knot" is old. The word knot, spelled cnotte in Middle English, has meant the bond of wedlock since the 13th century ([Gretna Green](https://gretnagreen.com/-a1015)). Several customs are offered as its origin, and the honest answer is that no single one owns it: the Roman bride's knotted belt, the British handfast, and a dozen more all point the same way.

What follows is a tour, culture by culture. For each one I will tell you what is tied, who ties it, and what the knot is said to stand for. At the end I will show you why the mathematics of knots says these people were right in a way they could not have known.

## Scotland, Ireland and the British Isles: the handfast

To handfast, in late Old English, meant to make a formal promise, especially a marriage contract ([encyclopedia.pub](https://encyclopedia.pub/entry/history/show/70990)). In medieval Scotland and the north of England the ordinary word for a betrothal was a handfasting: the couple joined hands and declared before witnesses that they meant to marry ([Celtic Life](https://celticlifeintl.com/?p=18792)). The term was still used that way in Elgin as late as 1635 ([Temple Study](https://www.templestudy.com/?p=420), citing A. E. Anton, *Scottish Historical Review*, 1958).

The picture most people now carry, a cord wound around the couple's wrists, came later. Nineteenth-century writers, starting from a story by Walter Scott and repeated by the historian W. F. Skene in 1837, read the old word as a literal binding of hands, and that is the form the ceremony took when it was revived ([USA Kilts](https://www.usakilts.com/blog/what-really-is-a-handfasting.html)). In the revived ceremony the hands are tied with ribbon or tartan, often one knot for each vow spoken ([Reader's Digest](https://rd.com/?p=1688147)), and sometimes all four hands are crossed so the cord makes a figure eight seen from above ([Temple Study](https://www.templestudy.com/?p=420)).

**What it stands for.** The joined hands are the promise; the cord is the witness that it was made. A knot tied in public cannot be quietly untied later.

**The true lover's knot.** In Scotland, England and Denmark, two interwoven overhand knots, each made of its own strand, were given as a love token ([Mama Lisa's World](https://www.mamalisa.com/blog/about-the-phrase-to-tie-the-knot/)). Each strand keeps its own knot, yet the two cannot be separated. That is the whole idea of a good marriage in one piece of cord: two selves kept, one bond made.

**The triquetra.** The three-cornered interlace of early Insular art, the one carved on stones and drawn in the Book of Kells, is drawn as a single unbroken loop that crosses itself three times. Mathematically that is the trefoil, the simplest true knot, and it will matter at the end of this article.

## Greece and Rome: the knot only the groom may loosen

A Roman bride wore a straight woven tunic tied at the waist with a woollen band, the cingulum, knotted in the knot of Hercules, the nodus Herculaneus. Only her husband was allowed to untie it ([Facts and Details](https://europe.factsanddetails.com/article/entry-1127.html)). The knot was tied that morning by the bride's mother ([Thalia Took](https://www.thaliatook.com/OGOD/cinxia.php)). The writer Varro says the groom untied it in silence, and the grammarian Festus names the knot itself ([Wikipedia, Weddings in ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weddings_in_ancient_Rome)).

The Hercules knot is what sailors call the reef or square knot: two strands, each passing over and under the other in turn ([Thalia Took](https://www.thaliatook.com/OGOD/cinxia.php)). It tightens under load and comes apart only when you know how.

**What it stands for.** Strength, because it was Hercules's; fertility, because Roman lore said he fathered some seventy children; and the bride's chastity, released only inside the marriage ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Flammeum); [Thalia Took](https://www.thaliatook.com/OGOD/cinxia.php)). A goddess, Cinxia, an aspect of Juno, was named for exactly this loosening of the belt ([Thalia Took](https://www.thaliatook.com/OGOD/cinxia.php)). The Romans also used the knot as a healing charm: Pliny says binding a wound with it makes healing "wonderfully more rapid" ([Wikipedia](https://wiki.riteme.site/wiki/Flammeum)).

Greek brides sometimes wore the same knotted girdle, and in Greek the word for knot could also mean a spell or charm ([Atlas Obscura](https://atlasobscura.com/articles/how-to-tie-historical-knots)). That double meaning, a knot as a binding that holds a power, runs through almost every tradition below.

**The Gordian knot.** The opposite lesson comes from Greek legend. At Gordium in Phrygia a cart was tied with a knot so tangled no one could find its ends, and it was said whoever loosened it would rule Asia. Alexander, in 333 BC, cut it with his sword. The story is remembered because it breaks the rule every wedding keeps: a true knot is answered by patience, not by a blade.

## Ancient Egypt: the knot of Isis

The tyet, also called the knot of Isis or the girdle of Isis, shows a length of cloth folded into a knot, a loop on top and the ends hanging down ([Art Institute of Chicago](https://www.artic.edu/digital-publications/42/ancient-egyptian-art-at-the-art-institute-of-chicago/173/cat-90-tyet-amulet-late-period)). It looks like an ankh with its arms bent down, and like the ankh it is often read as "life" or "welfare" ([Wikipedia](https://bafybeiemxf5abjwjbikoz4mc3a3dla6ual3jsgpdr4cjr3oz3evfyavhwq.ipfs.4everland.io/wiki/Tyet.html)).

Chapter 156 of the Book of the Dead calls for a tyet of red jasper to be placed at the throat of the dead, to give them the protection of Isis ([The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/570311)). Its words include: "You have your blood Isis, you have your power, you have your magic" ([Art Institute of Chicago](https://www.artic.edu/digital-publications/42/ancient-egyptian-art-at-the-art-institute-of-chicago/173/cat-90-tyet-amulet-late-period)). The Met notes simply that Egyptians believed knots bound and released magic ([SuperStock, Met record](https://www.superstock.com/asset/tit-isis-knot-amulet-ca-new-kingdom-tit-symbol-pronounced/6145-29768758)).

**What it stands for.** Not a wedding knot, but the oldest of the protective ones: the goddess's own binding, made small enough to carry. Red is its colour, as it is the colour of the cord in so many weddings that follow.

## India: three knots, seven steps, and the tied cloth

**The mangalsutra.** Mangal means auspicious and sutra means thread ([Sanskriti Magazine](https://www.sanskritimagazine.com/?p=7173)). In the ceremony called Mangalya Dharanam, "wearing the auspicious", the groom ties the sacred necklace around the bride's neck. On the wedding day a yellow thread, coloured with turmeric, is tied with three knots while the priest recites Vedic hymns; in some customs the groom ties the first knot and his sisters tie the other two ([Sanskriti Magazine](https://www.sanskritimagazine.com/?p=7173)). In parts of the south the necklace is called the thaali, and tying it confers the title Sumangali, an auspicious married woman ([My Gold Guide](https://www.mygoldguide.in/mangalsutra-–-sacred-thread-love)).

What the three knots mean is told differently by different families, and I give the readings side by side rather than pick one:

- As three commitments, to the husband, to his family, and to God ([Cultural India](https://www.culturalindia.net/weddings/wedding-traditions/mangalsutra.html)).
- As Manasa, Vacha and Karmana: believing it, saying it, and doing it ([Houghton Mosous](https://houghtonmosous.blogspot.com/2021/11/hindu-wedding-tying-knot.html)).
- As the three bodies of the person, the causal, the subtle and the physical ([En Route Indian History](https://enrouteindianhistory.com/?p=16766)).
- As two knots for the couple, tied by the groom, and a third for the bond between the families, tied by his sister ([Gems of Jaipur](https://www.gemsofjaipur.net/blogs/news/the-evolution-of-the-sacred-ornament-mangalsutra)).

In Andhra Pradesh and Telangana the pendant has two gold discs, one from each family ([Wikipedia](https://bafybeiemxf5abjwjbikoz4mc3a3dla6ual3jsgpdr4cjr3oz3evfyavhwq.ipfs.4everland.io/wiki/Mangala_sutra.html)). Two houses, one thread.

**The tied garments.** In many Hindu weddings the end of the bride's garment is knotted to the groom's scarf, a lifelong bond, and the knot is not untied until the ceremony is complete ([Mama Lisa's World](https://www.mamalisa.com/blog/about-the-phrase-to-tie-the-knot/)). Bound like this, the couple walk the saptapadi, the seven steps around the sacred fire, one vow for each step. In Hindu practice the marriage is complete when the seventh step is taken.

**The knot and the end of life.** The same tradition knows that a knot holds. It was once common, when someone was dying, to untie every knot in the room, so that nothing would keep them bound to life and suffering ([Atlas Obscura](https://atlasobscura.com/articles/how-to-tie-historical-knots)). A knot tied keeps; a knot loosened lets go.

## China: the red thread and the tied hair

**The red thread of fate.** In Chinese belief the god of marriage is Yue Xia Laoren, the Old Man under the Moon, Yue Lao for short. He ties an invisible red thread around the ankles of two people who are destined to marry; in the Japanese telling it runs from a man's thumb to a woman's little finger ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_thread_of_fate)). The best-known story is set in the Tang dynasty: a young man named Wei Gu meets an old man reading a book of fates by moonlight and is shown the child he will one day marry ([Introduction to Chinese Culture](https://fwdbook.qq.com/read/1033705932/8)). The thread can stretch or tangle, but it cannot break.

The thread entered real weddings. By the Tang dynasty it was already a custom for a red string to be given to the groom's parents and tied around the hands of the couple; in the Song dynasty a red handkerchief took its place ([Introduction to Chinese Culture](https://m.novel.qq.com/read/1033705932/8)).

**Jiefa, the tied hair.** A husband and wife of a first marriage are called jiefa fuqi, a "hair-tied couple". The saying records an old custom in which bride and groom each cut a lock of hair and tied the two together in one knot ([The World of Chinese](https://www.theworldofchinese.com/2021/03/choice-chengyu-symbolism-of-hair/)). The line that went with it, still quoted, is that once the hair is tied, love between husband and wife is free of doubt ([Rujia](https://www.rujiazg.com/article/31651)).

**Knots before writing.** Chinese decorative knotting, zhongguo jie, is made from a single length of cord woven into many shapes, each with its own meaning, usually in lucky red. It is believed to have begun as a way to record information and send messages before writing was in common use ([Wikipedia, Chinese knotting](https://kaweah.freedombox.rocks/kiwix/wikipedia_en_all_maxi_2023-10/Chinese_knots)). Among its eleven basic knots, the scholar Lydia Chen lists the double coin knot, which is the same knot as the European carrick bend, the Josephine knot, and the Japanese awaji knot ([Wikipedia, Chinese knotting](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Chinese_knotting)). One knot, found by three peoples, each calling it lucky.

## Japan: a knot that tightens when pulled

Japanese gift cords, mizuhiki, are tied in two families of knot, and the choice is a message. The bow knot, cho-musubi, comes apart when you pull its ends and can be tied again any number of times, so it is used for happy events you hope will repeat: a birth, a graduation ([Yamamotoyama](https://yamamotoyama.co.jp/en/blogs/column/reading284)). The knots that cannot be undone are kept for things that should happen once: weddings, recovery from illness, funerals ([Iyo Mizuhiki](https://fashiontechnews.zozo.com/en/kogei/iyomizuhiki1)).

A bow knot on a wedding gift is a faux pas, because its looseness suggests the couple might separate ([Zozo Artisan](https://artisan.zozo.com/en/articles/mizuhiki)), or marry again ([Japanese Wiki Corpus](https://japanese-wiki-corpus.github.io/culture/Mizuhiki.html)).

The wedding knot is the awaji-musubi, shaped like an abalone shell. Pulling on its ends only tightens it, so it stands for a long and lasting relationship ([Zozo Artisan](https://artisan.zozo.com/en/articles/iyomizuhiki3)). At a traditional wedding, white strings on the bride's breast are tied in a tight awaji-musubi that cannot unravel easily ([Niponica, Japan Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://web-japan.org/niponica/niponica29/en/feature/)).

**What it stands for.** Of all the traditions here, Japan states the mathematical idea most precisely: a marriage knot is one that strain makes stronger, and one that has no easy way back to loose cord. Odd numbers of cords are favoured because they cannot be divided evenly ([Sugimoto Tea](https://www.sugimotousa.com/blog/mizuhiki-and-the-art-of-gift-giving)).

## The Andes: the khipu, where the figure-eight knot means one

The Inka had no alphabet, and they ran an empire on knotted cords. Khipu is the Quechua word for knot ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Quipu)). A khipu is a main cord with pendant cords hanging from it, and on each pendant, clusters of knots record numbers in base ten: units, tens, hundreds and thousands, each at its own height on the cord ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/knotted-strings-and-inca-accounts); [MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Quipu.html)).

The rules are exact. In the tens place and above, a digit is that many simple overhand knots. In the units place, a digit from two to nine is a single "long knot" with that many turns. Zero is the absence of a knot. And the digit one, in the units place, is a figure-eight knot ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/knotted-strings-and-inca-accounts); [MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Quipu.html)). The Inka needed a different knot for one because a long knot with a single turn would look the same as a plain knot, and the end of a number would be lost.

The keepers of the cords, the khipukamayuq, could be summoned to court, where their records were accepted as proof of payments made ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Quipu)). Some khipu carry a top cord whose value is the sum of the cords beneath it ([Gresham College](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/sites/default/files/transcript/2021-10-20-1600_MEDRANO_BSHM-T.pdf)), and lower-level khipu add up, with small discrepancies, to matching cords on a higher-level one ([Plus Magazine](https://plus.maths.org/content/not-just-knots-secrets-khipu)). Knots were also tied in two mirror-image forms, S and Z ([Dialnet](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/4239603.pdf)), so the khipu even records which way a knot turns.

**What it stands for.** The khipu is not a wedding knot. It is something deeper: proof that a people trusted knots to hold the truth. A knot as a ledger, a record that cannot be quietly changed, with the figure-eight standing for the number one.

## Deeper into the khipu: the bills, the census, the letters, and the memory

The khipu deserves more than one section, because it is the one place in this article where a knot was not only a symbol of a promise but the promise itself, written down.

### The accounts

The makers and readers of the cords were the khipukamayuq, a Quechua name read as knot-makers, or knot-animators. Using cotton and camelid fleece, they kept the administrative books of the largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas: census counts and the allocation of tax ([Urton and Medrano](https://brewminate.com/the-khipu-code-the-knotty-mystery-of-the-inkas-3d-records/)). Spanish records say the cords tracked the census, what was in the storehouses, and tribute, which in the Inka state was paid mostly as labour ([ADP Rethink](https://rethinkq.adp.com/artifact-khipu-inka-accounting-system)). Other uses reported by the chroniclers include calendars and the organisation of the army ([Wikipedia](https://akarinohon.com/text/taketori.cgi/en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php%253Ftitle=Quipu&oldid=1028507964)). That the accounting cords follow a base-ten knot scheme, an abacus made of string, has been known for about a century ([Urton and Medrano](https://brewminate.com/?p=62555)).

This was a working ledger, not a keepsake. A top cord could carry the sum of the pendants hanging below it, and records from a lower level of the state were summed into the cords of a higher one, as I described above. After the conquest the Spanish Crown itself accepted khipus as legal documents in court cases and used them to bring Andean figures into the colonial books ([Dumbarton Oaks](https://www.doaks.org/resources/online-exhibits/written-in-knots-undeciphered-accounts-of-andean-life/post-inka)). A knot was a receipt. A khipu was an audited bill.

### The census that names a man

For most of a century scholars could read the numbers on a khipu but never who they were about. That changed with six cotton khipus said to have come from a single tomb in Peru's Santa River valley, now held in the Temple Radicati collection in Lima ([Wikipedia, Santa Valley quipus](https://wiki.krisyotam.com/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic_2026-03/Santa_Valley_Quipus)). Gary Urton had noticed that a Spanish census of the same region and period, a revisita of 1670, recorded numbers that matched those six khipus exactly ([Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/khipus-inca-empire-harvard-university-colonialism)). The census lists 132 tribute payers, and the khipus carry 132 corresponding cord groups ([Endangered Alphabets](https://www.endangeredalphabets.net/khipu/)).

In 2016 his student Manny Medrano, then a first-year undergraduate, spent his spring break working through the cords in a spreadsheet. He and Urton found that colour, and whether each cord was tied onto the main cord from the front or from the back, told you which of the two halves of the community, Hanan or Hurin, a person belonged to ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/?p=228697); [Google Arts and Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/khipu-from-the-santa-valley/UgFvS5UkgQ9-Mg)). Their paper in *Ethnohistory* (January 2018) calls it the first case of reading information from the attachment knots ([Harvard Gazette](https://content.news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/08/a-students-mines-voices-from-the-incan-past/)). Harvard wrote about one of those payers, a villager called Diego who had survived for three centuries only as an unnamed number on a cord ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/08/a-students-mines-voices-from-the-incan-past)).

The reading is still being argued over, which is how it should be. A 2024 paper in the same journal used combinatorics to propose a different best alignment of the six lineage groups to the two moieties ([Ethnohistory, 2024](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/asx__180491384)), and an independent computational check in 2026 reproduced the front-and-back structure itself ([arXiv 2607.00185](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.00185.md)). What survives every version is the idea I care about most: **which way a knot is tied carries meaning.** Front or back, S or Z. The hand of the knot is data.

### The letters

The Spanish chroniclers wrote that the khipus held more than numbers: histories, biographies, and messages between officials in different towns ([Sabine Hyland, Sapiens](https://sapiens.org/language/khipu-andean-writing)), and that relay runners, the chasquis, carried khipus as letters ([Hyland, via Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/incas-used-knots-communicate-textiles-201150234.html)). For a long time no surviving khipu could be shown to be one.

In 2015 the anthropologist Sabine Hyland of St Andrews was invited into the mountain village of San Juan de Collata, outside Lima, to see two khipus its people had guarded for generations in a wooden box, kept secret from outsiders, together with letters from the 17th and 18th centuries ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/inca-khipus-code-discovery-peru)). She was the first outsider allowed to see them, and only for 48 hours, under watch ([Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-inka-empire-recorded-their-world-in-knotted-cords-called-khipu-17945)). The village leaders told her what they were: narrative letters about a war, made by local chiefs during the rebellions of the 18th century ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/inca-khipus-code-discovery-peru)). Each one has more than 200 pendant cords, divided into groups by cloth ribbons knotted onto the main cord ([Hyland, Brewminate](https://brewminate.com/unraveling-an-ancient-code-written-in-strings/)).

Hyland counted 95 distinct signs in them, made from the colour of the cord, the animal the fibre came from, and the direction of the ply. That is enough signs for a logosyllabic writing system, and she read the final three-cord groups of each khipu as lineage names, in places telling fibre from fibre only by touch ([Archaeology](https://archaeology.org/?p=6146); [Current Anthropology](https://core.ac.uk/download/143472183.pdf)). One of the elders told her, in effect, that if they could read the box they would at last know who they really are ([Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-inka-empire-recorded-their-world-in-knotted-cords-called-khipu-17945)). Again, the direction of the twist is part of the message.

### The burning order, and what survived

In 1583 the Third Council of Lima, the church council that organised the Catholic Church in Spanish South America, ordered khipus recording the old religion destroyed as idolatrous ([Precolombino](https://precolombino.cl/wp/en/?p=35430)). The popular story that it banned and burned every khipu is too simple: the same council encouraged khipus to be made for confession and for memorising Christian doctrine ([Wikipedia, Third Council of Lima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Council_of_Lima)). So the cords were bent to a new use rather than ended. Communities went on keeping them. Hyland found villagers near Tupicocha who kept accounting khipus until the 1940s ([Sapiens](https://sapiens.org/language/khipu-andean-writing)), and in San Cristóbal de Rapaz a khipu is still guarded in the ritual precinct and revered as the village's constitution ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/unraveling-an-ancient-code-written-in-strings/)).

### The weavers

The cords did not come from nowhere. In the Inka world cloth was tax, gift, reward and currency, and the finest grade, cumbi, was the most precious gift of all; the best weavers worked up to 120 weft threads to the centimetre ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/791/inca-textiles/)). The most skilled women weavers, the aclla, were brought from across the empire to the House of the Chosen Women in Cusco ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/791/inca-textiles)). Most of that cloth was made on the backstrap loom, one end tied to a post and the other to the weaver's own body, so that she sets the tension by leaning ([Ancient History Sites](https://www.ancient-history-sites.com/?p=141765)). The same loom is still used in the Andes today ([Threads of Peru](https://threadsofperu.com/pages/peruvian-textiles-history)).

**What it stands for.** The khipu is the knot as memory. A promise, a payment, a census of real people, a letter in a time of war, kept in cord because cord could be carried, counted and checked. And it was made by the same hands that wove, which is where this story picks up again below, in a loom in Lyon and in a factory outside Boston.

## Spain, Mexico, the Philippines and Latin America: the lazo

After the vows in a Mexican Catholic wedding, the padrinos, sponsors chosen for the strength of their own marriage, drape a cord, rosary or ribboned lasso over the couple's shoulders in the shape of a figure eight ([The Wed](https://thewed.com/magazine/historical-cultural-symbolic-wedding-rituals)). The custom reaches back to medieval Spain ([mitú](https://wearemitu.com/wearemitu/culture/latino-wedding-traditions-explained/)) and is kept in Mexico, the Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Panama, Puerto Rico and Venezuela ([Love Stories TV](https://lovestoriestv.com/latinx-wedding-traditions)).

The sponsors place the loop on the groom's shoulder first and then the bride's, so that it forms the eight ([Catholic Faith Store](https://www.catholicfaithstore.com/daily-bread/?p=810)). In some weddings the priest wraps the couple's hands instead, binding them in a figure eight at the moment of the vows ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/global-weddings-156064366/)).

In the Philippines the same rite is the cord ceremony: cord sponsors drape a silk cord, the yugal, over the couple's shoulders in a figure eight, with the reverence given to a rosary ([Seiyaku](https://seiyaku.com/seiyaku/ph/jim-and-nila05.html)).

**What it stands for.** One loop around each person, crossing once between them. Each keeps their own circle; the crossing makes them one cord. It is read as infinity and as the shared yoke of the marriage, and in the Bible eight is the number of new beginnings ([Catholic Faith Store](https://www.catholicfaithstore.com/daily-bread/?p=810)).

## West Africa: the Akan knots of peace and wisdom

Adinkra are symbols of the Akan people of Ghana, each with a name in Twi and often a proverb behind it ([CSDT](https://csdt.org/culture/adinkra/origins.html)). Two of them are knots.

**Mpatapo, the knot of reconciliation.** Mpatapo means "pacification knot" and stands for peacemaking, forgiveness and reconciliation ([Adinkra Symbols](https://adinkrasymbols.org/symbols/mpatapo)). Where most cultures tie a knot to begin a bond, the Akan tie one to mend a bond that has been strained. In Albany, New York, it became the logo of SNUG, a group working against gun violence ([CSDT](https://csdt.org/culture/adinkra/origins.html)).

**Nyansapo, the wisdom knot.** Its proverb is "Nyansapo wosane no badwenma": a wisdom knot is untied only by the wise. The Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology carries the knot on its logo and the proverb as its motto ([Adinkra Symbols](https://adinkrasymbols.org/symbols/nyansapo)).

**What they stand for.** Between them, the two Akan knots say what the others leave unsaid. A bond can be tied again after it is strained, and a hard knot is not for cutting: it is for the patient and the wise.

## Tibet, India and the Buddhist world: the endless knot

The endless knot, palbeu in Tibetan and shrivatsa in Sanskrit, is one of the Eight Auspicious Symbols, the ashtamangala, of Buddhism, and is important in Hinduism and Jainism as well ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Endless_knot)). In Jainism it often marks the chests of the twenty-four tirthankaras ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Endless_knot)).

It is read as the interconnectedness of wisdom and compassion, the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth, and the interdependence of all things ([Tibetan Nuns Project](https://tnp.org/eternal-knot-symbol/)), and as the intricate, interpenetrating nature of reality and of the Buddha's mind ([University of Virginia, Tashi Takgyé](https://texts.mandala.library.virginia.edu/book_pubreader/39291)).

**What it stands for.** Where a wedding knot joins two, the endless knot says everything was already joined. It has no first strand and no last, and every crossing depends on every other. Mathematicians can name it too: the simplest interlaced form of the endless knot is the knot catalogued as 7₄ ([Wikipedia](https://bafybeiemxf5abjwjbikoz4mc3a3dla6ual3jsgpdr4cjr3oz3evfyavhwq.ipfs.4everland.io/wiki/Endless_knot.html)).

## Jewish tradition: knots that count

The tzitzit are the knotted fringes tied to the four corners of a prayer shawl. On each corner, eight strings hang down, held by five double knots, with four sections of winding between them ([Ohr Somayach](https://www.ohr.edu/3003)). In the common Ashkenazi tying the windings number 7, 8, 11 and 13 ([Jewish Link](https://jewishlink.news/torah-and-math-with-strings-attached)).

The knots are a counting device. In Hebrew each letter has a number, and the letters of tzitzit add to 600. Add the 8 strings and the 5 knots and you reach 613, the number of commandments in the Torah ([Chabad](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/537949/jewish/What-Is-Tzitzit.htm)). The windings speak too: 7, 8 and 11 make 26, the value of the divine name, and the last 13 is the value of echad, "one". Together the windings read "God is One" ([Jewish Link](https://jewishlink.news/young-professionals-tie-tzitzit-at-pti/); [Ohr Somayach](https://www.ohr.edu/3003)). This reading follows the commentator Rashi; Nachmanides objected that the biblical spelling of the word gives a different total ([New World Encyclopedia](https://newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Tzitzit); [Cavacopedia](https://cavac.at/cavacopedia/Tzitzit)).

**What it stands for.** A knot as a memory you wear: you look at it and remember everything you have promised.

**The broken glass.** A Jewish wedding has no binding cord. It ends instead with the groom breaking a glass, and the breaking is remembered, not swept away: even at the height of joy, a loss is kept. It belongs in this article because it is the same instinct seen from the other side. Some things are made whole by tying, and some are honoured by keeping the break.

## Sailors and folk knots: the knot that stops, and the knot that holds the wind

**The figure-eight stopper.** On a boat the figure-eight knot is tied in the end of a rope so it cannot run out through a block or fairlead, and among yachtsmen it is the most common stopper knot of all ([Practical Boat Owner](https://www.pbo.co.uk/nautical-almanac/glossary-of-nautical-terms/stopper-knot-8353)). Climbers use it the same way, to keep a rope from running out of a device ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure-eight_knot)). Its job is to keep the line from being lost. Hold that thought for the next section.

**Selling the wind.** In the folklore of the northern seas, witches and wizards sold sailors the wind tied in a cord. Frazer records the art of tying up the wind in three knots among wizards in Lapland and witches in Shetland, Lewis and the Isle of Man ([Frazer, The Golden Bough](https://sacred-texts.com/pag/frazer/gb00504.htm)). Undo the first knot and a moderate wind sprang up, the second half a gale, the third a hurricane ([Frazer](https://neonvagabond.xyz/mirrors/sacred-texts/pag/frazer/gb00504.htm)). A Scottish teller, recorded in Gaelic tradition, says plainly that the sailor was told he must not undo the third ([Tobar an Dualchais](https://tobarandualchais.co.uk/track/30322?l=en)). The Museum of Witchcraft and Magic in Boscastle, Cornwall, keeps such a rope, a white cord with three knots, from the trade in selling the wind on the north coasts of Devon and Cornwall ([Museum of Witchcraft and Magic](https://museumofwitchcraftandmagic.co.uk/?p=43219)).

**What it stands for.** A knot can hold more than a rope. It can store a power and release it in measured steps. This is the oldest folk statement of what a knot is: something kept, which comes back out only in the order it went in.

## The loom: how weaving learned to remember

Every cloth is made of two sets of threads: the warp, stretched lengthwise on the loom, and the weft, passed across it. At every crossing the weft goes either over a warp thread or under it. That is a choice between two things, made thousands of times, and a pattern is nothing but the record of those choices. Over and under is also exactly what a mathematician records at each crossing of a knot diagram. The loom and the knot speak the same language.

### The drawboy

To weave a pattern in silk, the old drawloom needed a second worker, often a child, the drawboy, who sat above the loom and lifted the right warp threads for each row by hand, reading from a design drawn on squared paper ([Book of Science](https://sharpgiving.com/thebookofscience/items/p1822b.html)). The pattern lived in the drawboy's head and hands. When he made a mistake, the cloth kept it.

### From paper tape to cards

In Lyon, the silk city, a weaver named Basile Bouchon built a mechanism in 1725 in which needles were raised or lowered according to whether a hole appeared for each needle in a strip of perforated paper. His assistant, Jean-Baptiste Falcon, arranged the holes in rows on rectangular cards and joined the cards together in a loop ([Book of Science](https://sharpgiving.com/thebookofscience/items/p1822b.html)). Jacques de Vaucanson, famous for his mechanical duck, then tried to automate the whole loom with Bouchon's needles and Falcon's cards, and the weavers, who saw their living in danger, pelted him with stones in the street ([Book of Science](https://sharpgiving.com/thebookofscience/items/p1822b.html)).

### Jacquard

Joseph Marie Jacquard was born in Lyon in 1752 into a family of silk weavers, in a city where the silk trade employed some 40 per cent of the workforce. He patented his first loom on 23 December 1800, and his card-driven loom was working by April 1805, when Napoleon made it public property ([History Today](https://www.historytoday.com/node/57538)). Jacquard replaced Vaucanson's drums with punched paper cards; each card passed before an array of metal rods, one for each warp thread ([Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-french-connection/)). Where a card had a hole, the rod went through and lifted its thread; where there was no hole, the rod was stopped ([Hand or Eye](https://handoreye.com/shop/p/jaquard-cards)). The cards were laced together with string, one card for each row, and the drawboy was no longer needed ([Science History Institute](https://sciencehistory.org/distillations/the-french-connection)).

A drum can only repeat; a chain of cards can be as long as you like, so there was no limit to the patterns a Jacquard loom could weave. One person could run it, and patterned cloth came off it more than twenty times faster than before ([Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-french-connection/)). The same cards travelled the world: sets of Jacquard cards were still driving looms in the Nishijin weaving district of Kyoto ([Hand or Eye](https://handoreye.com/shop/p/jaquard-cards)), in the country whose wedding knots appear above.

### The portrait, Babbage, and Lovelace

To show what the loom could do, the Lyon firm of Didier Petit wove a portrait of Jacquard in silk using about 24,000 cards ([Science History Institute](https://sciencehistory.org/distillations/the-french-connection)). The English mathematician Charles Babbage owned one and liked to show it to guests; in it, Jacquard's hand rests on a stack of punched cards ([History Today](https://www.historytoday.com/node/57538)). Babbage saw that the cards that told a loom which threads to lift could tell a calculating machine which operations to perform, and he planned his Analytical Engine to be driven by them ([Science History Institute](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/the-french-connection/)).

In her notes of 1843 on that engine, Ada Lovelace wrote the sentence every history of computing quotes: "We may say most aptly that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves" ([Lovelace, quoted in Zenodo, *Weaving as Binary Art*](https://zenodo.org/record/831627/files/weaving_as_binary_art_AOM.pdf)). That paper adds a fair warning: Jacquard did not invent the algebra of patterns, which weavers had carried for millennia; he built the first widely used machine that fed it in on cards ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/record/831627/files/weaving_as_binary_art_AOM.pdf)).

**What it stands for.** A hole or no hole. Over or under. The loom turned the weaver's memory of a pattern into a thing you could hold, stack, copy and run again, the same thing the khipu had done with numbers. The card is the knot's cousin: a record that keeps.

## The women who wove the memory that went to the Moon

The computer that guided Apollo 11 to the Moon in July 1969 carried its program not on a disk and not on a chip, but sewn by hand into wire. It was designed and programmed at the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory and built by Raytheon ([Wikipedia, Core rope memory](https://bafybeiemxf5abjwjbikoz4mc3a3dla6ual3jsgpdr4cjr3oz3evfyavhwq.ipfs.4everland.io/wiki/LOL_memory.html)), and the sewing was done by women at workbenches in Raytheon's factory in Waltham, Massachusetts ([Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/apollo-software-woven-core-memory/)).

### Core rope: a wire through a ring

The memory was made of tiny rings of iron ferrite, the cores, each about a millimetre across ([Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/apollo-guidance-computer-woven-memory/)). A copper wire passed through a ring meant one; a wire passed around it meant zero ([Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/the-computer-that-landed-apollo-11-stored-its-code-in-rope-memory-that-was-physically-woven-by-hand-by-a-workforce-of-women-in-a-massachusetts-factory-a-wire-through-a-magnetic-ring-meaning-one-a/)). In this design the cores were not switched on and off as in ordinary memory; each worked as a small transformer ([Wikipedia](https://bafybeiemxf5abjwjbikoz4mc3a3dla6ual3jsgpdr4cjr3oz3evfyavhwq.ipfs.4everland.io/wiki/LOL_memory.html)), so a wire threaded through the ring picked up a pulse of current when that core was driven, and a wire that went around it stayed quiet. The memory was read back as electricity.

Because up to 192 sense wires could thread a single core, one ring held 192 bits, twelve 16-bit words ([Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/apollo-guidance-computer-woven-memory/)). The Block II guidance computer carried 36,864 sixteen-bit words of rope in about one cubic foot, against 4,096 words of ordinary erasable core memory in two cubic feet, roughly eighteen times the density ([Wikipedia](https://ipfs.ssi.eecc.de/ipns/en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Core_rope_memory)).

It was memory in the khipu's sense, not the computer's: read-only. Once a module was woven the software could not be patched; a module took about eight weeks to make, and the women hired for it came from the textile and watch industries ([SpaceDaily via Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/spacetech/news/apollo-11s-guidance-software-couldnt-be-patched-at-the-last-minute-it-was-woven-with-copper-wire-by-0e7dc61c-390f-4554-ae65-602808735937/)). The work was so critical to the mission that it was tested and inspected several times over ([Science News](https://sciencenews.org/?p=3110578)). The historian David Mindell put it simply: every bit had to be sewn with a needle ([Sisters in Making](https://sisters-in-making.shorthandstories.com/f8f33e9d-d430-4bfd-9642-1f506d505b5d/sisters-in-making/sisters-in-making/)).

### The little old ladies and the rope mothers

The engineers called it LOL memory, for Little Old Ladies ([Science News](https://sciencenews.org/?p=3110578)). The nickname was paternalistic ([Meyersign](https://meyersign.com/?p=2460)), and historians still disagree about whether it was widely used during the programme or attached to the work afterwards ([Make Tech Easier](https://maketecheasier.com/the-computer-that-landed-apollo-11-stored-its-code-in-rope-memory-that-was-physically-woven-by-hand-by-a-workforce-of-women-in-a-massachusetts-factory-a-wire-through-a-magnetic-ring-meaning-one-a/)). The Smithsonian records that the software leader Margaret Hamilton herself called the weavers LOLs ([Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/rope-mother-margaret-hamilton)). The engineers who oversaw each program's rope were called rope mothers ([Science News](https://sciencenews.org/?p=3110578)); many of them were men, and the rope mothers' own boss was Hamilton ([Amusing Planet](https://www.amusingplanet.com/2020/02/that-time-when-computer-memory-was.html?m=0)). A recent project names the women by every title they were given, from weavers and technical assistants to keypunch operators, and calls them the sisters in making ([Sisters in Making](https://sisters-in-making.shorthandstories.com/f8f33e9d-d430-4bfd-9642-1f506d505b5d/sisters-in-making/sisters-in-making/)).

I want to say plainly what the nickname hides. These were not little old ladies doing handicraft. They were skilled textile workers, hired for the precision of their hands, holding in their fingers the only copy of the program that would land two men on the Moon. One wrong pass of the needle, through instead of around, and a one became a zero.

### The Navajo women of Shiprock

Far from Boston, in the desert, another group of women built Apollo's machine. From 1965 to 1975 Fairchild Semiconductor ran an integrated-circuit plant in Shiprock, New Mexico, on Navajo land ([Nakamura, *American Quarterly*](https://just-tech.ssrc.org/?p=8659)). At its peak it employed more than a thousand Navajo workers, most of them women ([Democratic Underground, quoting the Fairchild brochure](https://democraticunderground.org/10386322)), and among what they assembled were integrated circuits for the Apollo Guidance Computer ([Science News](https://sciencenews.org/?p=3110578)).

Fairchild's 1969 brochure set a photograph of a Navajo rug beside a circuit and called the women's skill innate ([Computer History Museum](https://computerhistory.org/blog/indigenous-circuits/)). The scholar Lisa Nakamura has shown how that language cast the women as naturally suited to the work by culture and gender, rather than as skilled workers to be paid as such ([Nakamura](https://just-tech.ssrc.org/?p=8659)). The plant closed after a protest occupation in 1975, and Fairchild moved the work to Asia ([New Savanna](https://new-savanna.blogspot.com/2017/03/the-role-of-navajo-women-in-early.html)).

The likeness the brochure used is real all the same, and a Navajo weaver has taken it back. Marilou Schultz, a Diné weaver and a mathematics teacher, weaves computer chips into traditional tapestries. Her *Replica of a Chip* (1994) points to the intertwined history of Navajo women and semiconductors ([Bard College](https://www.bard.edu/news/survey-marilou-schultz-navajo-weaving-digital-technology-ccs-2026-02-02)); in 2024 she wove a work to honour the women of the Shiprock plant, with spiders in its border for Spider Woman, who in Navajo tradition gave the people weaving ([American Craft Council](https://craftcouncil.org/?p=29054)). She builds her patterns thread by thread as she weaves, not from a plan laid out in advance ([documenta 14](https://www.documenta14.de/en/artists/22610/marilou-schultz)).

**What it stands for.** The oldest idea in this article, carried to the Moon. A khipu says one with a figure-eight knot; a core rope says one with a wire through a ring. Both are memories made by hand, both are read by touch or by current, and both cannot be quietly changed once tied. Twice in history, a whole civilisation's most important record was trusted to the hands of weavers, and most of those hands were women's.

## What the mathematics of knots says

Here is where my own work comes in. In mathematics a knot is a closed loop of cord with its ends joined, so it can never be slipped off; a knot is "true" when no amount of moving it in space will turn it back into a plain circle. Every people above found by hand the facts that mathematicians later proved. I have spent these past months checking several of them line by line on a computer, with a proof checker called Lean that accepts nothing it cannot verify. Here is what the cords already knew.

**The simplest true knot is the three-cornered one.** The triquetra of the Insular stones and manuscripts, drawn as one interlaced loop, is the trefoil, the simplest knot there is ([Wikipedia](https://cavac.at/cavacopedia/Triquetra)). It has three crossings, as the mangalsutra has three knots.

**It has a hand.** A left-handed trefoil can never be turned into a right-handed one, however you move it. The Inka tied their knots in two mirror forms, S and Z; the mathematics says that for the trefoil the two really are different knots. I have this proved: no change of basis can carry the trefoil to its mirror image, because its defining form is negative everywhere. A vow that only turns one way cannot be reversed and called the same promise.

**The figure-eight is the knot that sees itself in the mirror.** Unlike the trefoil, the figure-eight knot can be turned into its own mirror image, and I have the explicit move that does it, checked. It is also the knot of the lazo, the khipu's number one, and the sailor's stopper that keeps the line from being lost.

**Two stones become one space.** The space around a figure-eight knot can be built from exactly two perfect pieces, two regular ideal tetrahedra, glued face to face. This was shown by the mathematician William Thurston. Each piece, on its own, contributes a minus one to the gluing; together, minus one times minus one, the edge closes to one. Two become one, literally, in the geometry.

**The vow that cannot be mirrored is the plan of the house.** The shape of those two pieces is set by a single equation, z² − z + 1 = 0, whose answer is a sixth root of unity: turn it six times and you are home. The trefoil's own twist, the way it carries its surface once around, obeys exactly the same equation and comes home after six turns. On the trefoil's "ladder" the numbers run 2, 1, −1, −2, −1, 1, 2: down through the dark middle and back to where they began on the seventh step, like the seven steps around the fire. So the knot that can never be its own mirror holds the blueprint of the knot that can.

**The broken piece is kept.** The same gluing equation has a second, rejected answer: flat pieces built on the golden ratio, which fail the test for a perfect tetrahedron by exactly one sign. In my work this broken answer is not thrown away. Squared, it becomes the stretch of the figure-eight itself, and after five turns that stretch measures 123, a number that also turns up in a continued fraction for 6/(3−π) found by the Ramanujan Machine group. Like the glass broken at a Jewish wedding, the break is kept, and it turns out to be the ruler.

I want to be careful about what I am and am not saying. I am not claiming the Inka or the Hindu priests or the Celtic monks knew topology. I am saying that every one of these traditions chose the knot, of all things, to stand for a promise that holds, and the mathematics now proves that knots have exactly the properties they trusted: some can never be undone, some have a hand that cannot be reversed, and the simplest pair of them, the trefoil and the figure-eight, are bound to each other by one equation. Eighty separate statements of this work are machine-checked, and that is the knot work I will be sharing next.
